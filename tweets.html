Nvidia −14% then +33%: why Lance Breitstein's capitulation rule says skip a gap like this (Cavco win, Snap loss)
另一面
@OtherSideBJ
↗
Last Friday Nvidia fell 14% in a day; this Monday Jensen Huang returned to his post, and it rose 33% right at the open. This kind of crash followed by a sharp reversal is Lance Breitstein's specialty. Lance has made over $100 million trading, and this year's new book in Jack Schwager's Market Wizards series has a chapter devoted to him. He only buys the bottom after one kind of crash, called a capitulation decline: when the degree of panic exceeds the cause of the panic. The decline suddenly steepens, each candle is longer than the last, the price falls far below the lower Bollinger Band, and volume surges. Even when it falls like that, he doesn't buy while it's falling. A crash and reversal looks like a V; he waits for the right side of the V, i.e. the price turns up and rises above the previous day's high, then buys. The stop goes first at the low that just formed, and is then moved each day to the previous day's low. Let's look at a few examples. Manufactured-housing maker Cavco fell for a week in late January, then crashed another 20% on January 30 on more than 4x normal volume. On February 4 it rose above the previous day's high and he bought, with the stop 7.5% below the entry. The stop moved up each day after that; it wasn't broken until February 17, when he sold for a 17% gain. Snapchat parent Snap fell 6 days in a row in early February, the last day down 13%, with volume also surging to 4x normal. On February 10 it rose above the previous day's high and was bought; the next day it broke the stop for a loss of about 3%, and then fell nearly another 30%. Back to Nvidia. Last Friday was a capitulation decline, but on Monday it opened 16% above Friday's high. The stop would have to go at Friday's low, a quarter below the entry price, versus only 7.5% on the Cavco trade. Lance said in early November that stocks have often been closing very weak and then gapping straight up the next day; that kind of market is the most dangerous, and he would rather miss it than buy the bottom early.

Lance Breitstein's right-side V reversal: how he made ~$10M buying the 2024 Nikkei crash, applied to Nvidia now
另一面

↗
Lance Breitstein has made over $100 million trading, and his specialty is buying the bottom in crashes. On August 5, 2024, the Nikkei crashed 12.4%. That day he bought Nikkei futures and made about $10 million on one trade. Last Friday Nvidia fell 14% in one day and the Nasdaq fell 5.3%. Many people are again wondering whether to buy the dip, so his method may offer some ideas. His method is the right-side V reversal: in a crash, don't guess the low; wait for the price to turn and form the right side of the V, then buy. The most common way to bottom-fish is to start buying when it feels like it has fallen enough, buying more as it falls. Lance did that when he started, and lost money almost every month of his first year. Later he realized the same price has completely different odds on the left and right sides of the V. Buying on the left, the price is still falling, there's no sensible stop, and you can only hold on. Buying after it turns, the stop can go at the low that just formed and the win probability is much higher, so on the right side he dares to use a bigger position. Next I'll explain his rules step by step using his classic Nikkei trade. [1/5] Step 1: wait only for real panic; look at how the price falls. Lance picks bottom-buying chances not by how much it fell but by how it fell. If candles get shorter, the decline gets gentler and volume shrinks, the market is slowly accepting lower prices; he doesn't touch that kind of steady decline. Real panic: each candle longer than the last, the slope of the decline steeper and steeper, price falling below the lower Bollinger Band, volume suddenly surging. When three or more big red daily candles appear in a row, he starts paying attention. He only picks the most extreme cases, after several waves down, in instruments everyone is watching; entering on every small bounce just produces one small loss after another. From July 11 to 26, 2024, the Nikkei fell from 42,224 to 37,667, over 10%, but most days fell only 1–2% on normal volume, so by his standard it was a steady decline. On August 1, 2 and 5 it fell three days in a row, accelerating from 2.5% to 5.8% to 12.4%, on volume 1.5x, 1.7x and 2.3x the 50-day average. On August 5 it closed nearly 9% below the lower Bollinger Band, its biggest one-day drop since 1987, falling faster than during COVID. [2/5] Step 2: don't buy on the left side of the V; wait for a turn signal. In a panic the price can fall anywhere. Lance doesn't guess the bottom; he waits for the price to turn on its own. His three common turn signals: price breaks above the previous candle's high; in a steep decline, price breaks the downtrend line; in a gentler decline, price reclaims a moving average. That night on the Nikkei, he first made the mistake of buying on the left. August 4 was a Sunday; after futures opened that evening the Nikkei kept plunging, down 3%, 5%, 10%. Near the circuit breaker it looked like it was turning, so he bought heavily, but price fell back to the low and he took the loss. This time, though, the Nikkei didn't make a new low and quickly reclaimed the low it had just made. To Lance, that was the signal to wait for, and he went heavily long, one of the largest positions of his career. [3/5] Step 3: stop at the low that just formed; add after a higher low is confirmed. Buying on the right side gives a clear stop: the low that just formed; breaking it means he was wrong. With a clear stop, Lance dares to use a much bigger position than usual. In one example he gave, with the same profit and loss room and a higher win rate, the trade's expected return became 4x the original. After entering he waits for a second confirmation. The Nikkei bounced, broke its descending trend, then pulled back; he needed to see whether this pullback held above the previous low. That half hour felt like days. Once the higher low formed he kept adding, and also went heavily long the Nasdaq, buying until his broker warned of insufficient buying power. Then the Nikkei fell back near the low and his open profit nearly vanished; by his rules, breaking the low meant a stop that would lose millions. Price held. After the US open stocks rallied, and he also bought Nvidia, Apple and Bank of America. [4/5] Step 4: trail the stop up under the previous day's low; after a big rally, lock in most of the profit first. His exit is also set in advance. The stop starts at that day's low, then moves to the previous day's candle low, moving up daily; he exits when price breaks the previous day's low. When the move is especially fast he sells a large part in pieces first, because the higher it goes, the farther from the stop, and the expected return of holding shrinks. 24 hours later Nikkei futures were back to Sunday night's opening level. He sold most of the position in a few big chunks, locking in about 80% of the profit. The remaining 20% trailed the previous day's low until the Nikkei, Nasdaq, Apple, Nvidia and Bank of America had all recovered the losses of those days, then was stopped out. On the Nikkei daily chart, this stop triggered on August 21, about 20% above the August 5 close. He made over $10 million on this trade. [5/5] The benefits: a clear stop and high odds, allowing bigger size. The cost: you never buy the exact low, and the turn signal can fail; that night his first trade on the Nikkei was stopped out. It also works only in a few extreme panics; most declines aren't worth waiting for. Applying the four steps to Nvidia: in October it fell slowly, with short candles and normal volume, a steady decline. In November the red candles got longer and several days closed below the lower Bollinger Band. Last Friday it fell 14% in one day with a 13% range on 3.5x the 50-day average volume, so the panic of Step 1 has appeared. But that candle closed near the day's low, so the right side of the V hasn't appeared yet. By his rules, wait until price stops making new lows and reclaims the low, or breaks above the previous candle's high of 143.73, to count as a turn, with the stop at the low of this move.

Swedish trader Kristjan Kullamägi blew up his account three or four times in his first two years of trading, each time with only $2,000–5,000 of capital. He says he has made money every year since 2013, with a 268% annualized return over his first 7 years. He uses only three kinds of entry. The first is the breakout: after a strong stock moves sideways for a few weeks, buy when it rises above the highest price of the sideways range. Three things to check before buying. First, it made a move in the past 1–3 months, rising 30% to 100%+. Second, it then consolidates for 2 weeks to 2 months, with each low higher than the last, the price hugging the rising 10-day and 20-day moving averages. Third, buy when it rises above the consolidation high, with the stop at the low of the day you bought. Selling happens in two steps. 3–5 days after buying, sell a third to a half first, move the stop on the rest to the buy price, then hold along the 10-day moving average and sell the rest only on the first close below it. Chip designer Arm did this once this year. It rose 50% in two and a half weeks, moved sideways for 3 weeks, and on April 17 broke above the consolidation high: bought at $167.34, stop 2.8% below the entry. On the 3rd trading day, sold half for a 17.5% gain. On May 4, the first close below the 10-day moving average, sold the remaining half for a 21.5% gain. Copper company Freeport-McMoRan rose 60%+ in two months, moved sideways for 4 weeks, and on February 25 broke above $69.44: bought, and the next day it fell through the stop for a 1.9% loss. By March 19 its low was 27% below the buy price. With Arm, half was sold on the 3rd trading day for 17.5%, and the stop on the other half was moved to the buy price; from that day on, even if the price fell back, the trade as a whole was profitable.
