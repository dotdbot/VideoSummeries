Laid off as an AI engineer (don't make my mistake)

📺 Michael Hoff 📅 November 2, 2026 ⏱️ 9:33 ▶ Watch on YouTube
TL;DR: Michael Hoff was laid off from an AI startup that focused on ServiceNow, where he wrote deterministic, compiler-like code. Looking back, he lists five mistakes: (1) he dismissed AI after it failed on one hard problem and never re-tested it; (2) he argued with colleagues who said "AI will get better" when he should have prepared for it; (3) he ignored the speed gains because he disliked AI-written code, while teammates shipped more features; (4) he held onto the craft of coding, even for throwaway scripts; (5) discouragement turned into a shame loop. What saved him was not AI skill but his boring, specialized ServiceNow background, and he has never been out of work for more than a month. His advice: use the tools, and specialize in a niche with real barriers to entry, then add AI skills on top.

🏢 Background: His Role at the Startup

  • He worked at an AI startup built around ServiceNow, a large enterprise platform that companies use for IT tickets, workflows and internal apps.
  • He held several roles: first a ServiceNow consultant converting applications mostly by hand, then an engineer building tools to automate those conversions, and later a forward deployed engineer working directly with clients.
  • His main project let a user draw a diagram of an application (a "universal application notation") and turned it into a working ServiceNow app. He wrote the deterministic code that produced the ServiceNow pieces. No AI was involved in that step; he compared it to a compiler.

❌ Mistake 1: Not Using AI, Even at an AI Company

  • At the time, AI tools weren't consistent enough to produce the exact XML ServiceNow needs for update sets (how changes move between environments). AI-generated update sets were broken and didn't work in a live environment.
  • He decided AI "wasn't enough for real work" and stopped paying attention. That was the actual mistake.
  • He wasn't wrong about the tools then. He judged AI on one very hard problem and never checked again as it improved.
"You don't have to bet your whole workflow on it, but you do have to keep testing it."
  • Fix: set aside a small part of each week to try new tools, even on tasks where you expect them to fail.
  • This mistake wasn't fatal by itself, but it led to all the others.

❌ Mistake 2: Ignoring People Who Said "AI Will Get Better"

  • Some teammates said, "We don't need to think too hard about this part; AI will get better and handle it." He strongly disagreed, arguing that if AI got that good, none of them would have jobs.
  • The irony: he predicted the outcome correctly and said so to his team. But he used that prediction as a reason to dig in instead of a reason to prepare. He treated it as an argument to win, not a warning.
"Not liking the direction doesn't change the direction."
  • Fix: take your own prediction seriously, check it regularly, and become the person on the team who knows how to use AI, not the one arguing against it.

❌ Mistake 3: Ignoring the Speed-Ups

  • He disliked AI-assisted code from colleagues: "crazy band-aids", lots of repetition, odd structures bolted on.
  • He was proud of his own code and understood every line, but it took a long time to write. Meanwhile, teammates shipped many more features.
  • The business cares about what ships and whether it works for the client, not how much you like your code. When deciding who to keep, the person shipping is safer than the one with "the prettiest pull requests".
  • Fix (meet in the middle): use AI to get a working version fast, then apply your standards to reviewing and cleaning it up instead of writing every line by hand.
  • Speed was what the company could actually see, and it was the one thing he wasn't giving them.

❌ Mistake 4: Holding Onto the Craft

  • Much of his resistance was personal: he liked writing code and solving hard problems himself, and letting AI do that felt like giving away the part of the job he cared about.
  • The evidence: as a forward deployed engineer, he mostly wrote temporary scripts for clients that nobody would maintain. That's an ideal use case for AI, with no architecture to protect, and he was still slow to adopt it. So the code-quality argument no longer applied.
  • He calls this the real reason behind the first three mistakes.
  • Fix: separate the craft from the job. Keep hand-coding your own projects if you enjoy it, but at work the job is solving the problem.

❌ Mistake 5: Letting It Get to His Head (the Shame Loop)

  • As the job changed, he got discouraged about his skills and "wallowed in it", feeling that what he was good at no longer mattered.
  • The loop: feeling down → avoiding new tools because you don't want to be bad at something again → staying in your comfort zone → falling further behind → feeling worse.
  • Fix: treat the feeling as information, not a verdict. Feeling behind means you are behind, and being behind is fixable.
  • If it's affecting your life beyond discouragement, talk to someone "instead of watching 10 more YouTube videos".

🛟 What Actually Saved Him

  • Not his AI skills, but his "boring" background: most of his ~10 years in tech were in ServiceNow, and little of it was in a formal software engineering role.
  • Because of it, he landed on his feet quickly and has never been out of work for more than a month.
  • The reason is less competition: ServiceNow is specialized. Employers expect certifications and real platform experience, and few people have both.
  • General software jobs, by contrast, are "a really crowded line", since anyone with an AI subscription can now do parts of that work.
