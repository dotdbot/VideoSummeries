🧭 The Thesis

  • Clients keep asking where to buy productive, affordable agricultural land before everyone else gets there. US farmland "has gone way up in value", is "largely owned by big corporations now" and is no longer a great deal.
  • He's looking for land that might come with a second residence or a passport, a place to go "if times got tough or there was a war", and an asset that, "unlike a bank account, can't just be taken from you".
  • Price gaps between neighbors:
    • Paraguay vs. Uruguay: up to 80% cheaper, because Uruguay "convinced the Europeans to move in" and is more stable. He recently did an agricultural tour of Paraguay.
    • Rwanda vs. Uganda: Rwanda is "premium priced" (he praises the gorilla tour). Similar land next door in Uganda costs "much, much less", though the political risk differs. Foreigners can't own land everywhere.
  • How his approach has changed: he has bought land for over a decade. At first he bought small plots for residence permits and long-term appreciation, which was "almost speculation". It worked: he recently sold land near the airport in Georgia at about a 20% IRR. Now he wants land he can do something with.
  • His point is that land should be able to re-rate. Malaysia is a great place to live, but land there isn't cheap. In Cambodia, cheap land plus agricultural yields that are "ostensibly as good" means a better percentage return, with room for other uses later.

🇰🇭 #1 Cambodia: Passport First, Then Land

  • He recently became a Cambodian citizen and asked how to make the citizenship investment useful so "the passport itself isn't just speculation". He has a stack of passports, each with a strategic value. Cambodia's value is emotional: he has loved the country for 20 years.
  • He owns land in Kampot and is expanding a Kampot pepper plantation (white, red and black pepper, which sells at a premium). They plan other uses on the land later to make it more profitable.
  • Passport pricing: Cambodia is "talking about raising the price of this passport" because they see the value. Wealthy Asian buyers (e.g. "some wealthy Chinese guy") want countries where they can own large tracts. His advice: "if you like Cambodia, I would look at the passport now."
  • Land types: pepper land is among the most expensive. There are cheaper up-and-coming areas in eastern Cambodia, maybe some of the best timber land in Asia, and some tourism potential. They saw a parcel in the eastern provinces for 28 cents/m².
  • Non-citizens can own land through a trust structure that has replaced the old informal nominee arrangements. You pay for it every year, "so you better be generating money with that land."
  • Choice: buy in the controlled regions and export pepper, or grow trees.
  • Road risk: land must connect to roads. Ask whether roads are planned for upgrade in the next 5–10 years, before, say, your 20-year trees are ready to harvest.

🇵🇾 #2 Paraguay: Cheapest Land He'd Fully Trust

  • "Potentially some of the most affordable land anywhere in the world," in "the one country in South America that's always pretty much been pro-business." It's also very tax-friendly.
  • Eastern timber plays sit on a corridor connected to Brazil. They've looked at land for 20–30¢/m² near eastern cities where exports to Brazil are possible. It may not be ideal land.
  • Chaco land: 10¢/m² or less for grazing land about 2 hours outside Asunción. It's mainly for cattle, and he doubts that leasing it to graziers is very effective.
  • He's thinking about building homes for buyers on Paraguayan land and asks interested viewers to comment.
  • As in most of Latin America, you don't need citizenship to buy. Buying can lead to a residence permit, and if you live there and learn Spanish you can apply for naturalization. Land and citizenship are "separate plays".
  • He calls it "probably the most affordable place that I would fully trust". South America is easiest if you can't or don't want to get citizenship first. They're "actively looking at some stuff there right now."

🇪🇨 #3 Ecuador: Very Cheap, More Difficult

  • Ecuador just elected a more pro-business government after going back and forth. The downside is drugs: "Ecuador has become the hub," more than Colombia, and violence is up. Expats selling may create buying opportunities.
  • A trusted contact did a ~10-day tour last month across several regions.
  • Southern Ecuador: very affordable, but check how much is usable. A 50-hectare (~125-acre) plot might have ~40% or ~40 ha sloped (the transcript is unclear). Mostly sloped land can cost ≈25¢/m² or less, "like 2 cents a square foot". The slopes give privacy. It's prettier than Paraguay: rivers, monkeys, banana trees.
  • Manabí province (coast): good for cocoa, with affordable areas near a major airport that has flights to Panama. It could also be a place to live.
  • Amazon: interesting but harder to use. Small individual farm plots cost around $50,000 for "a whole bunch of land", but that's less interesting at scale.

🇦🇷🇩🇴 #4 Argentina and #5 Dominican Republic

Argentina

  • A friend just bought land there: "It's so incredibly cheap… how could I not do well?" This is more speculative.
  • There's lots of Chaco land, wine land (vineyards look like "a heck of a lot of work" to him) and good grazing land. Sometimes it's 10¢/m²: "not the best land", but "a floor you can go up from."
  • Risks: there's so much land, and it's unclear "what happens to Argentina next". Paraguay is the more secure historic story. He cites Doug Casey: "I'm just the rich gringo they leave alone here… I own my land."

Dominican Republic

  • Probably not something he'd do himself and not for most people. It suits people who want a home or work in hospitality.
  • He thinks the less-developed parts of the Samaná Peninsula are the next place to grow, compared with popular, well-connected Punta Cana. North of Samaná they saw beachfront at ~$20/m², though not all of it is that cheap.
  • He compares it to beachfront deals in Nicaragua and El Salvador. He saw land near San Juan del Sur, Nicaragua this year (not on the water), but he's unsure about Nicaragua's future and says it's not as affordable as Ecuador. He wouldn't take on that risk.

🇿🇦 #6 South Africa (and Why Not Namibia)

  • He knows this pick may get pushback because of negative publicity. Land is incredibly cheap, but much of it is grazing, low-productivity or timber land. There are wide-open areas in the south-central region, not far from Stellenbosch or Cape Town. He'd happily spend a week "staring out into the abyss" there.
  • "South Africa is very open": nearly anyone can buy, and you can build. It's more open than Morocco, which people also mention for its light restrictions.
  • At the Africa CEO Forum, South Africans told him they want business. It's very pro-Global South, and a place where he'd "rather go with a non-Western passport". That's one reason he thinks an African passport will be valuable.
  • For hospitality, there's interest in Tanzania, but foreigners can't own land there.
  • Namibia is a backup ("the Paraguay to South Africa's Argentina"), but it's "going in the wrong direction" and wants to restrict land ownership. Large agricultural land needs permission. The Namibia Stock Exchange CEO, whom he interviewed on his separate podcast Borders, had convinced him about the country.

🇯🇵 #7 Japan: Lifestyle, Not Business

  • Village land is incredibly cheap "because nobody wants it." Immigration has historically been hard and labor isn't cheap. "Who wants to live out in these villages and farm?"
  • It works if you buy a house, live there and run your own small farm, but not as a business. He loves Japan but wouldn't do this. He's interested in the other six to some degree.

🔁 Passports Buy Land, Land Buys Passports

  • He uses a holistic strategy, asking how one decision affects another (company location → taxes → residence). Second passports and residence permits aren't just places to live or travel documents. They also give "better investment positioning".
  • "Buy signal" on an African passport, because African countries keep opening up to other Africans:
    • São Tomé and Príncipe has a citizenship program, and he might add it to his land list once his application goes through.
    • Egypt offers citizenship for $300,000 in real estate that you can sell after 5 years. He calls it a "call option".
    • Botswana keeps talking about a program, but none has appeared yet.
    • Whether this will let you buy land in other African countries: "I don't know," but he likes the trend.
  • Reverse direction, the "unlisted passport": if you buy enough land and show plans (jobs, a hotel), a government official may recommend you for citizenship through legitimate channels, e.g. "the governor recommends you to the president who has the legal authority to sign off". He stresses it's "not cash in an envelope".
  • He sees chances in EU and non-EU European countries, in Cambodia "through the royal channel" and in South America, creating a cycle where "the passport could facilitate the land and the land could facilitate the passport." He also expects that once you've developed land in one country, you can pitch the next government on doing the same there.
"Don't hold dead land, and use a passport to reach land you otherwise couldn't." (from the video description)

✅ Takeaways and Checklist

  • Look for cheap neighbors of premium countries (Paraguay vs. Uruguay, Uganda vs. Rwanda) and price in the political risk.
  • Buy land with a productive use (pepper, timber, cocoa, grazing, hospitality), not dead land.
  • Check usable area (slopes), road access and planned upgrades, title quality and ownership structure costs (Cambodia trust, Bulgaria company).
  • Latin America mostly doesn't require citizenship to buy. Parts of Asia and Africa do, or need structures, so a passport can unlock them.
  • Watch the policy direction: Namibia is tightening, South Africa is open, and Cambodia may raise its passport price.
