🚀 Ambition and What Students Build

  • Ambition (or "entrepreneurial efficacy", agency) has no playbook. What works: ask "what could you do more than that?" and put people among more ambitious peers. Before AI you stopped because you could only do so much; now the frontier keeps moving.
  • Right after ChatGPT launched, he showed it in class — and one student coded his entire semester project during that class.
  • His latest class included surgeons, biomedical-device people, finance people, NGO workers and soldiers — all now building real things: mapping a company's full supply chain and its financial weak spots, explaining medical information to patients beyond forms. Narrow problems they know deeply.

Startup ideas that fail every year

  • Replacing real-estate brokers — looks like easy disintermediation, but brokers are woven into social, political and legal systems.
  • Student-only problems — home-cooked food delivery, storage between semesters — less universal after college.
  • They aren't bad ideas — there's real demand — just harder than they look. "Whoever cracks it makes a lot of money." (Marina points to DoorDash and Airbnb as counter-examples.)
  • On "big problems take the same effort as small ones": aim big from a blank slate — ambition attracts the best people — but scaling (Series B/C) is a different skill set from running a local business.

⛏️ Is It Still Worth Starting a Software Company?

"You don't want to sell picks and shovels. You want to dig a gold mine with new gold-mine technology."
  • In the Industrial Revolution, the people who got rich weren't selling steam engines. If you can make a business more efficient, do it yourself — overcome the barriers directly.
  • Good news for entrepreneurship overall, but everyone is too "SaaS-brained", and SaaS is under threat. Software is becoming table stakes for doing other interesting work; he wants founders to make or do something, not just sell software.
  • Everyone's a coder: Anthropic studies suggest managers now write better first-pass code than coders, because it's about clear instructions.
  • Every company has SaaS vendors in its sights and wants to build its own tools — though OpenAI and Anthropic reportedly still use Slack and Workday. Companies are realizing many startups were selling them LLM capabilities they already have.
  • Software will be democratized: for non-technical MBAs, the old key to success was "find a technical person". Now software becomes a toolkit, alongside things like payroll — he once calculated employee taxes in Excel each week at his first startup, not knowing a service did it for cents.

How to develop taste

  • See lots of good and bad examples and learn to pull them apart. He brings an art professor into class to teach how a designer sees (what makes a font or image good).
  • Build language for taste, like a film buff or a VC with taste in a problem space. Taste means your taste.
  • He avoids the word "judgment": AI obviously makes judgment and creative calls in a 17-hour task. The question is whether they match yours. Example: mathematicians now stress the aesthetic value of proofs — a proof that teaches nothing beautiful has little value.

🏚️ Why Junior Jobs Stopped Training People

  • Universities face a temporary AI crisis with a known fix: AI is a great tutor if used as one (cheating teaches nothing, even if you think it does); more action learning and in-class tests.
  • The real problem: taste and judgment were learned at work through apprenticeship — 4,000 years of white-collar training. Juniors did grunt work cheaply to prove themselves; managers gave feedback.
  • That broke: an intern's incentive is to deliver the best product — so they hand over Claude/ChatGPT/Gemini output without understanding it. Managers ask why give feedback to a slow human when Claude can do it.
  • Companies' choice: the easy path — hire only seniors (early signs of this) — or the hard path — make training a real part of jobs, allow inefficiency, maybe add a year of employer-specific training (a "Google year" or "McKinsey year").

"Cognitive surrender"

"We can easily give up skills to the AI, and we may never get those skills back. So we have to purposely add friction to our lives in certain key areas so we maintain an advantage."
  • "Desirable difficulties": if it doesn't feel like mental sweat, you're not learning — like exercise for the brain.

📈 "Good Enough" Professionals and Experts

  • Jagged frontier: AI is surprisingly good at some things and bad at others. While it stays jagged, humans fill bottlenecks — e.g. lawyers interact with human, legal and social systems.
  • "Good enough" is a bundle of skills. A mediocre coder with strong product vision or communication may thrive — though the valuable skills will keep "ping-ponging".
  • AI used to be mainly a leveler (helping weak performers). Since agents (~last year), studies including Anthropic's show experts get the biggest gains.
  • Also: "AI whispering" — a theory-of-mind skill for how a model thinks — plus a mysterious third factor.
  • Negotiation-agent study (Alex Imas et al.): small differences in agent-building skill have huge impact because agents run many negotiations; good agent builders aren't necessarily good negotiators; and while women tend to underperform in human negotiations, women built much better negotiation agents than men.

💡 Should AI Generate Your Ideas?

  • He's skeptical of "map your own thinking process into a workflow". Better analogy: delegating to a team and knowing where teams go wrong.
  • He types every first draft himself (Substack, X, everything). Otherwise AI "will absolutely dominate what I'm thinking" and you read Claude filtered through him. His best ideas come during SoulCycle.
  • Brainstorming lesson: invented ~1955, known to be flawed by ~1957 — groups anchor on the first idea. Generate ideas individually first, then together. Same with AI: use it for ideas only after you've generated your own.
  • Equal-odds rule: people who generate more ideas generate more good ones; creativity is a trait. If ideation isn't your strength, let AI help — we lionize ideas too much; execution and emotional connection matter.
  • Testing ideas is now at "rock-bottom cheapness" (lean startup, MVPs, seed rounds all grew from cheaper testing). AI is a decent first-pass simulated customer, but not a real one.
  • Ideas alone were always a dime a dozen; the original idea rarely survives six months. Value comes from execution.

🤖 How to Manage AI Agents

  • "The revenge of the MBAs": technical people now need management skills. Wharton's lab finds techniques that work on people work on AI.
  • The hidden cost isn't tokens but the effort of monitoring work. When specifying agent tasks he defines: check-in points, where to give him diverse options to choose from, clear descriptions of good and bad, and he watches early for miscommunication — "Claude has its own obsessions".
  • Biggest mistake: trusting agents too much. Interfaces increasingly hide chain-of-thought to feel like magic, which is "disastrous" for keeping a human involved. You must create forced points where the AI reports back.

His real workflow: the "shape of stories" research

  1. Ask the agent for many possible approaches — don't let it execute its favorite.
  2. Have it speculate on how it would approach each; give feedback, like with a student.
  3. "Execute 1, 2, 3 and 4 as first drafts and show me."
  4. "I like what you're doing with 3, but 2 has a nice feature — how do we combine them?" and "This looks wrong — show me why."

A Socratic, choice-point process: the AI does the tedious work (finding 5,000 stories, building coding tools); he keeps the interesting work. It's a management meta-process, not a fixed workflow, so new models don't break it. Otherwise "anyone could type the same sentence to get the same result."

"An automation world will be bad for everybody in a lot of ways, but an augmentation world is like the dream of work."

📋 What He Hands Off — and Where He Draws Lines

  • Fact-checking is "superhuman": on his new book the AI found his errors and, more often, errors in his sources — a wrong table in an academic paper, the wrong city in an AP story.
  • Busywork: school and immunization forms, NDAs, certain legal documents — skills he "can't wait to deskill" himself on. Mandatory training? "Not yet."
  • Marina's story: her AI assistant "Dot" filled in and sent a form she only meant to have filled. Mollick's AI once emailed his dean after he said not to.
  • His lines: he runs Dot plus scheduled Codex tasks that summarize his world, but writes personally to anyone he has a real connection with; AI drafts rejections for strangers. He tunes system prompts over time and uses agents for low stakes before high stakes.
  • Concern: magical products with hidden system prompts remove the trust-building process — like building a human team. New model generations (e.g. Opus 5.5 vs. 4.6) feel different and must be re-trusted; companies retrain 500 people and the tool changes overnight.
  • Yet organizations "muddle through": no top-5,000 US company went under from COVID. LLMs are so effective "almost all their sins are forgiven".
  • AI reading his book raised deep cross-chapter questions about human autonomy that no human reader had — and now he feels obliged to address them.

🔮 Mindset, Local Models and the Future

  • Mindset: excitement and curiosity, plus acknowledging that nervousness, resentment and imposter syndrome are real — "everyone's making this up", including the labs. Focus on how much more you can do, with play and experimentation. Half of professors want to quit; half find it the most exciting time ever.
  • Local models: he calls "50% of enterprise tasks local by 2030" a made-up number. Enterprises don't want models on every machine. Don't fixate on today's problems — e.g. specialized legal AIs were overtaken by better general models. "Ride the exponential."
  • Set goals AI can't hit yet ("unsaturated personal benchmarks"): his are a high-taste academic paper from scratch (AI papers are correct but not interesting), vibe-coding a true startup from an idea, a game from scratch, and a coherent story he'd want to read.
  • If AI learns taste? Then he has nothing useful to say. But jaggedness keeps being underestimated, and taste means your taste — AI sameness ("Claude websites") will tire people. Add your taste and AI does incredible things.
