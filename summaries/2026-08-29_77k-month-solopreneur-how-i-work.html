⏰ The Daily Routine — Same Day, 365 Days a Year

  • 6–7 AM
    Wake up naturally — no alarm. The body wakes itself up after solid sleep. Coffee and breakfast with his wife.
  • Morning
    Gym with his wife — currently training for Hyrox. Sled push, squats, running. Non-negotiable.
  • ~9 AM
    Deep work begins. Phone completely off. No emails. No social media. No internet. Opens code editor only. Works for 4–6 hours straight.
  • ~3 PM
    App/feature launches happen here — tweet, LinkedIn, Threads, Reddit simultaneously.
  • ~4 PM
    Goes online. Checks Twitter, emails, handles support, does the "boring" creative-light work.
  • 5:30 PM
    Dinner — usually long, watching Better Call Saul. Winds down.
  • Evening
    Walk. Phone off. Computer off. No work talk. Sometimes a movie or reading before bed.
  • 8–9 PM
    Wind-down routine: dim the lights at home for 30–60 minutes before sleep. Falls asleep instantly.
  • 9 PM
    Bed — every single night at 9 PM. "It's a bit religious."
"I love my day so much that I want to repeat it every single day. I don't like Sundays. My fun is actually making stuff." — Marc

🧠 The Deep Work Block — Why Morning is Sacred

  • 4–6 hours of zero distraction every morning — this is the non-negotiable core of the entire system. Everything else is built around protecting it.
  • Why no social media in the morning: AI is moving so fast that checking feeds triggers FOMO — "I'm not fast enough, I should do more." One hour disappears. Motivation drops. The morning is ruined.
  • No email in the morning: If he reads an email about a broken feature, he'll spend the morning on bug fixes instead of building what truly matters. Reactive work kills creative output.
  • He only opens one thing: his code editor. That's the entire workspace for 4–6 hours.
  • The phone — "this little boy here" — is described as "the worst enemy." Off. Not silent. Off.
🔑 The Core Principle
Deep work is "what got me here." If I want to go further, I must keep protecting it. Everything else — support, emails, social — comes after the creative block is complete.

🎲 The 5% Hit Rate — Why Shipping Volume Is the Strategy

  • 35 startups shipped. ~30 of them = nothing. No users. No revenue. No traction. That's a 5% hit rate.
  • The math is simple: "If you roll the dice once, nothing crazy will happen. But if you keep playing, playing, playing — at some point one roll will work. You'll have happy users, you'll start to make money, you can leave your job, and then roll the dice more often."
  • Marc has never met a single person who said "I've tried 10 times and it never worked." He's met hundreds who have a brilliant idea stuck in their head or on a hard drive, never shipped.
  • Shipping is the only way to validate an idea. Not user research. Not surveys. Not beta lists. A live product with a buy button.
  • Every new ship teaches you more. Every launch grows your audience a little. Every failure gives you new ideas for the next thing.
"I cannot recall a single person who told me: 'Hey, I've tried 10 times and it never worked.' Just remember to keep shipping." — Marc

🛠️ What He Builds — Two Types of Products

💡
Type 1: "This must exist" He spots a personal problem, decides the solution should exist, builds it. No revenue projection. No market sizing. Example: Posture Pal — a macOS app that analyzes posture via webcam and alerts when you're slouching. Built because he spends all day at a computer. Day-1 revenue: ~$1,000. Users started posting about it on Twitter immediately.
🔍
Type 2: "This sparked my curiosity" He sees a gap or an interesting idea online, gets curious, builds it. Example: Trust the Mual — started from a Peter Levels tweet about people faking revenue online. Marc built a leaderboard of verified revenues. It evolved into a marketplace now doing $35K+/month.

How He Decides What to Build Next

  • He maintains a massive to-do list. "Even if I live 200 years, I wouldn't be able to make all my ideas real."
  • After shipping something, he scans the list and asks: "Which one has the most impact on users? Which one would make me happiest?" Then just picks it.
  • Every day he ends with more items on his list than he started with. Feedback, user requests, new observations — all fuel new ideas.

🤖 The Controversial AI Take — "The KPI Hasn't Changed"

  • Marc has a "slightly controversial take" on AI productivity: most people are obsessing over agent setups, multi-threaded workflows, teams of AI running in the background — but the single KPI that matters hasn't changed.
  • The only KPI that matters: number of things you ship. That's it. Same as before AI.
  • His own AI setup: "The most boring setup ever." One code editor. One chat window on the right. Talks to the AI, it builds a feature, he ships it, moves on.
  • He's shipped 300 features on his marketplace in the last 3 months and 6 new apps in 2026 — with this simple, single-threaded approach.
  • "People fall for the productivity trick" — spending hours optimizing their AI workflow instead of shipping. You could do a lot with the first version of ChatGPT and a basic code editor.
💡 The Insight
Complexity in your tooling is often a proxy for avoiding the real work. Shipping is the work. A simple setup that ships daily beats an elaborate agent system that ships monthly.

😴 Sleep — "The Most Underrated Thing"

  • For the first ~10 years of his adult life (ages 18–28), he was always sleepy. Would fall asleep mid-task at his computer.
  • Once he fixed his sleep, everything changed: emotional stability, locked-in focus for 4+ hours without breaks, genuine energy throughout the day.
  • The system: dim lights 30–60 min before bed → same bedtime every night (9 PM) → no alarm → body wakes itself at 6–7 AM naturally.
  • "When you go to bed, you fall asleep instantly." That's the goal — not forcing sleep, but engineering the conditions for it.
  • "Sleep is really underrated. I think it's so important." Not a hack — the foundation.

🚫 The Biggest Mistake — Emotional Attachment to a Single Project

  • Pattern he sees constantly: someone ships an idea, gets a few users and a little revenue, and it becomes their "pet project." They pour everything into it. They stop shipping new things.
  • The problem: that project might take 3 years to reach income-replacement level. But a new idea could do it 100× faster.
  • Staying attached to one slow-growing project means missing the next one that might explode.
  • The solution: keep rolling the dice. Don't treat any single launch as your one shot. Treat shipping as a permanent practice, not a one-time event.
  • Each new launch grows your audience, your skills, and your portfolio of ideas. The compounding effect is in the habit of shipping, not in any single product.
