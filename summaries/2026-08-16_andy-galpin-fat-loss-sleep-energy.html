😴 Sleep — The Biggest Undiagnosed Disease

  • Sleep apnea is a massive blind spot. 70–80% of people with clinical sleep apnea go undiagnosed; for women it's 90–95%. Roughly a billion people globally have it — and that's before counting insomnia or other disorders.
  • Prevalence by group: middle-aged men 24–34%, women 9–17%, seniors 30–60%, post-menopausal women ~25%.
  • Apnea isn't just "big and fat." Fluid shifts when you lie down (larger bodies carry more fluid, which pools in the neck), neural causes, positional causes, and anatomy all play a role — small, thin people get it too.
  • Snoring is a strong indicator, not a diagnosis. Apnea doesn't always wake you up, but it's linked to long-term death risk, cognitive decline, mood, recovery, and metabolic health.
  • Diagnosis path: free validated questionnaires first (RLS, insomnia, apnea, circadian, chronotype) → $100–600 wearables/home tech → sleep hospital study if you have insurance and a real flag.
  • Wearables are good for trends, bad for absolutes. Total sleep time = decent. Deep/REM sleep and "sleep quality score" = unreliable. The one metric worth trusting: "did I feel worse today?" — then reverse-engineer why.
  • Why sleep is getting worse while spending on it rises ($600B/year, yet -60 min average sleep over 60 years): the world is physically brighter (NASA satellite data + "skyglow"), global always-on business hours, urbanization (anxiety/depression up 20%+ moving to cities), constant screens.
  • Morning light exposure counteracts evening light damage — light earlier in the day sets circadian rhythm and reduces the harm of bright light exposure before bed.
  • Sound matters as much as anything. Target ~35dB rural vs. 60–70dB urban baseline. Sound machines: most people run them too loud/close (40–50dB when sub-40dB is better, especially for kids). Inconsistency (AC kicking on/off) disrupts sleep more than steady volume.
  • Falling asleep to a podcast/TV = a coping mechanism for an overstimulated brain, not a neutral habit. Better fix: solve the root cause (can't downregulate cognitively) with chamomile, magnesium, or a real wind-down routine instead of masking it.
  • Sleep supplement evidence tiers: Tier 1 (strongest): magnesium (bisglycinate form), chamomile/apigenin, melatonin. Tier 2–3: tart cherry juice, 2–3 kiwis before bed (genuinely studied and repeatable), omega-3.
  • Melatonin is a hormone, not a sedative — and is badly misused. Effective dose is ~0.3–0.5mg (not the common 5–10mg sold). It resets circadian rhythm (useful for jet lag) — it does NOT help if you wake up mid-night, because you're already in rhythm. Some tested pills contain up to 100x the labeled dose, causing next-day "hyper-dosed" grogginess (sleep inertia).
  • Temperature: 64–68°F is the general target; cranking AC too cold can itself disrupt sleep via the on/off noise cycle and dirty-filter air quality issues.
  • Sleep divorce is real and backed by data — people sleep worse sharing a bed. Practical middle ground: separate sheets/blankets so one partner's movement doesn't roll the other, earplugs, a pillow barrier.
  • Postpartum sleep disruption is one of the largest — and most neglected — causes of long-term sleep disorders in women; care shifts entirely to the baby right after birth.
  • Goal isn't "optimize sleep," it's sleep resilience — the ability to have one bad night and still perform, rather than needing a 12-step nightly ritual to function.

☕ Caffeine — What It Actually Does

  • Caffeine is an ergogenic (performance-enhancing) aid, especially for endurance — but per meta-analysis it does not restore you to your fully-rested performance ceiling after sleep debt. It brings you back to baseline, not to "rested + caffeinated" peak.
  • Practical takeaway: you can't out-caffeinate poor sleep and expect peak performance — you'll feel fine but perform worse than a well-rested, caffeinated version of yourself.

📱 Screens & the AI Attention War

  • The real sleep-disrupting danger of screens isn't blue light — it's arousal from the search for novelty (a puzzle, a scroll, a "what's next"). TV, tablets, phones, and books can all be equally disruptive depending on content engagement.
  • Bartlett connects this to Meta's Q3 earnings call: Mark Zuckerberg described feeding every post through an LLM that understands full context (not just captions/hashtags), boosting retention (+15 basis points), and previewed "Muse" — AI-generated, hyper-personalized content nobody made, tailored to what keeps a specific user (e.g., "Andy") scrolling at 11pm vs 9am.
  • Framed as an evolutionary trap: humans are wired for novelty and variable rewards (classic pigeon-lever experiment) — unpredictable dopamine hits (every ~40th scroll) are more addictive than predictable ones.
  • Practical defense: physically separate yourself from your phone (charge it in another room; don't touch it for the first hour of the day).

⚡ Energy — What Separates High Performers

  • Top performers don't work harder — they're better at energy management: choosing what activities/relationships give energy vs. drain it, and cutting the 80% effort that only returns 20% ROI.
  • Two buckets to diagnose low energy: visible stressors (not exercising, under-eating, overusing stimulants, poor sleep) and hidden stressors (undiagnosed pathogens — e.g. an MLB player's fatigue traced to mold exposure; energy toxicity from over/under-fueling).
  • Exercise builds energy via more/larger/healthier mitochondria and increased total blood volume/red blood cells (more oxygen delivery) — the SAID principle: Specific Adaptation to Imposed Demand.
  • "Exercise" is a modern, narrow, artificial category — historically humans got the same adaptation from carrying, climbing, sprinting, grappling. Any consistent physical stress (walking, gardening, pickleball) can produce equal clinical outcomes for mortality, joint pain, body composition.
  • Behavioral prescription: the fix for low energy is usually just ONE changed constraint per week, not five — because stacking too many changes causes people to abandon all of them.
  • Moderate/light exercise generally gives energy back; max-intensity sessions deplete it short-term — but on aggregate, regular training raises daily energy. When sick/traveling/busy, do the light version; when healthy, push intensity for long-term adaptation.

💪 Strength, Grip & Power — What Actually Predicts Health

  • Grip strength is a useful, cheap (~$40 device) crude health marker — but it's been overhyped as "the" mortality predictor. If you already have full blood work and body comp data, a high/low grip score adds little new information.
  • Grip strength tests both muscular force and nervous system/brain signaling — not just "arm strength." Grip asymmetry (notably different left vs. right) is now studied as an early marker for neurological conditions (Alzheimer's, Parkinson's).
  • Power (force × velocity) is distinct from raw strength and matters separately — "powerpenia" (accelerated power loss with age, beyond normal muscle-strength loss/"sarcopenia") predicts fall risk and fracture risk, especially past 60–65.
  • Training frequency is less important than weekly volume. As long as total weekly training volume is equal, how many days you split it across matters less than believed.
  • Shorter rest between sets to "build more muscle" is not well supported by evidence. 60–90 seconds (up to ~2 min) rest generally lets you produce a higher-quality next set — and quality (technique, range of motion, mind-muscle connection) beats cramming in more reps with poor rest.
  • Having any structured plan matters more than the specific plan. Progressive overload (more reps, sets, load, or range of motion week over week) is the actual driver — an AI-generated basic program is "good enough" to get a true beginner started for 6–10 weeks.

🎯 Fat Loss — Categorically Simple, Practically Hard

  • There's no magic method — no single food to avoid or one "correct" way to train. Fat loss is simple in principle (create a caloric deficit) and hard in practice, so the real skill is picking a strategy that fits your personality.
  • "Baker vs. cook" personality framework: Bakers need precise measurement (weigh/measure everything, daily weigh-ins, exact rules — no deviation). Cooks work better with 1–3 loose concepts (e.g. "protein + fresh veg at every meal," or an eating window) and less daily tracking. Most people actually want the "baker" precision because it removes decision fatigue — even though they think they'd prefer loose concepts.
  • Recommended sequence: do the precise "baking" approach for 2 weeks first (weigh/measure everything) to calibrate your intuitive sense of portions, then relax into looser tracking once your eye is calibrated.
  • Diet-recall research on grad students: people consistently underestimate fat intake (think 20% of calories, actually ~40%), underestimate carbs eaten, and underestimate protein intake. No food is inherently "evil" — the issue is total calories, not the macro itself (saturated fat isn't evil, it's about context/quantity).
  • Exercise's role in fat loss is real but overstated as the primary lever. Machines overestimate calories burned; the body can also downregulate other daily energy expenditure (adaptive thermogenesis) to offset workout calories burned. But exercise during a diet reliably improves long-term fat-loss maintenance (keeping weight off for years), even if it's not the biggest short-term lever.
  • Galpin's 5-4-3-2-1 rule for fat loss:
    • 5 — be active 5 days/week (any movement/vocation counts, rest days are fine)
    • 4 — 4 days/week of structured exercise (doesn't all have to be max effort — a yoga day counts)
    • 3 — at least 3 of those sessions hard enough to genuinely sweat
    • 2 — ideally 2 days/week of strength training
    • 1 — at least 1 day/week with a real high-heart-rate effort
  • "Nothing's changing despite going to the gym" is almost always a lack of a real training program, not a lack of effort — 80% of the time the fix is simply having any structured, progressively-overloaded plan instead of "winging it."

🔑 What Actually Triggers Behavior Change

1
Acute health shock or diagnosis. A scary result forces action — but the threat of death alone rarely changes behavior at scale (otherwise nobody would smoke or stay obese).
2
A close, personal wake-up call. A parent's heart attack, a friend or neighbor dying — proximity matters far more than statistics. "Too close to home" is what actually moves people.
3
Seeing your own quantified data. People dismiss a stranger's cholesterol numbers but freeze up seeing their own. Andy cites a pro athlete who insisted he was fast until force-plate data proved otherwise — then bought in completely.
4
Major life transitions — becoming a parent, a landmark birthday, retirement. The most common one Andy sees: selling a company. A form of "post-exit depression" where health becomes the new project/purpose after losing the built-in excuse of "sacrificing for the company."
5
Redefining what "success" even means. Bad goals (e.g. "go to the gym every single day, forever") set people up to quit after one missed day. Better framing: ask what success and failure actually look like — losing weight might be secondary to the shoulder pain disappearing, energy rising, or digestion normalizing.
