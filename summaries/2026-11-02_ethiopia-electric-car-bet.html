🚫 The Gas Car Ban (2024)

  • The country moving fastest on EVs isn't Norway, China or California. Ethiopia is poor, doesn't make cars, and about half the population still lacks reliable electricity.
  • In 2024 it banned imports of new petrol and diesel vehicles — the first country to fully ban imports of fuel cars. No cars were confiscated; gas cars already in the country stay on the road, but no new ones can come in.
  • The UK, Canada and EU members made similar pledges, but spread over a decade, and many have been quietly walked back. Ethiopia did it "almost overnight".
  • Dealerships in Addis Ababa now sell only EVs.
"What happens when a country decides it can't afford gasoline anymore?"

💸 Follow the Money: Why Ethiopia Did It

  • "Forget climate for a minute. Follow the money." Ethiopia imports more than it exports, and fuel is one of the biggest imports — up to $5 billion a year.
  • For years it subsidized fuel with money it didn't really have. In 2023 it defaulted after missing an interest payment on its bond — the latest default in Africa.
  • The IMF stepped in with a $3.4 billion bailout.
  • Fuel was hard to get: in Addis, queues at gas stations lasted hours and sometimes days.

A taxi driver's maths

  • Natanel Taddesse, a taxi driver in Addis Ababa, switched to electric. He saves about $150 a month out of a total income of about $480 a month. (His own words are in Amharic and are missing from the transcript; only the narrator's numbers remain.)

⚡ Step One: Cheap Electricity

  • Average electricity price is about 10¢/kWh, compared with about 18¢ in the US and 40¢ in the UK last year.
  • The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile was inaugurated last year. It produces about 5,000 MW, the largest hydroelectric plant in Africa. It took 14 years and about $5 billion, and a long fight with Egypt over water rights.
  • 99% of electricity comes from clean sources (96% hydropower). Ethiopia makes enough to export power to Tanzania, Kenya and Djibouti.

But getting power to people is the problem

  • Population is about 130 million; only ~55% have access to electricity. Blackouts are common.
  • When the ban started there were almost no public charging stations.

🔌 Chargers: Cars Came Before Chargers

  • In the West, a fossil-car ban with no fast chargers would cause "shock and horror". It's the classic chicken-and-egg problem: no chargers means no EV buyers, and no EVs means no one builds chargers.
  • In Ethiopia most people charge at home, and public chargers came after sales took off — "the chicken first and the egg came later."
  • There are now about 200 public charging stations, spreading to cities such as Adama and Hawassa.

🇨🇳 Chinese Cars and the "Flat-Pack" Tariff Ladder

  • Nearly every EV on dealership lots is imported from China, mostly Chinese brands.
  • About five years ago China barely exported vehicles. Now it's one of the world's largest vehicle exporters, ahead of Germany and Japan. "If the Ethiopian story feels like a sideshow to Western automakers, the global figures should worry them."

🏭 BKG's Minibus Factory

  • The government has licensed about 17 EV assembly companies, but only one, BKG Manufacturing, has started operating. It is about 30 km outside the capital.
  • Belayneh Kindie Group started in 1991 as an import-export business. It's now one of Ethiopia's largest private conglomerates (hotels, farms, construction) and has made vehicles since 2018.
  • It builds electric minibuses, the shared taxis used across Addis. Most people don't own cars. Big buses belong to the city; minibuses are run by individuals.
  • Parts come from China's Nanjing Golden Dragon Bus Company. At first Chinese engineers ran the factory floor. On a second visit, Ethiopian workers had been fully trained and ran every process themselves.
  • Many minibuses go to private drivers and companies, but the biggest single buyer is the city.
  • The plant builds about one vehicle a day. BKG is building a second plant for passenger cars, starting assembly in 2027.

🎯 Ambitions vs. Affordability

  • 2025 climate plan targets for 2033: 500,000 EVs and 2,000+ charging stations.
  • Sticker price is the main obstacle. A small EV like the BYD Seagull costs about $23,000. Average yearly income per person is under $1,000.
  • So Ethiopia has one of the world's highest EV shares and one of its lowest car-ownership rates: 13 cars per 1,000 people vs an African average of 73.
  • Supply is also a problem: starting a car industry is "a tall order", and current output is far below the target.

⚔️ Conflict and Geopolitics

  • In September 2026 fighting broke out again in the northern Tigray region, almost four years after a peace deal ended the civil war between the government and the Tigray People's Liberation Front.
  • Ethiopia accuses Eritrea, Egypt and Sudan of backing the rebels; all three deny it. Ethiopia is increasingly isolated in the region, partly because Egypt and Sudan opposed the dam.
  • The prime minister has made access to the Red Sea a national priority, fueling speculation about Eritrea's port of Assab.
  • The Iran war / battle for Hormuz pushed crude to its highest level since 2023, with oil prices up 40–50%. EV sales in developing countries have grown much faster since the war started.

🔮 What's Next and the Takeaway

  • Another hydropower plant is under construction and due by the end of the decade.
  • In 2027 Ethiopia hosts the UN Climate Summit in Addis Ababa and wants to show what an EV transition looks like in a developing country.
  • The model doesn't simply copy to other countries. Most don't have a river like the Nile, a car market small enough to switch this quickly, or a fuel crisis big enough to force the issue.
"The question was never really whether a country could do this fast, it was whether one would. Rich countries have spent two decades planning for a future they keep postponing. Ethiopia couldn't afford to wait, so it didn't."
