📮 800 Rejections and Two Big Traps

Trap 1: You are not the customer

  • Steve Jobs already has a Mac. A calculus teacher already knows calculus. Teaching people who don't know it yet is empathy.

Trap 2: Authenticity is overrated. Be consistent.

  • "No one wants you to be authentic other than your friends." With everyone else, be a professional: make a promise and keep it, whatever mood you're in.
  • "We already have a Kardashian. We don't need another Kardashian." Being in front of the camera is a lottery with lousy odds.
  • "Keanu Reeves is not Neo." He just knows how to act like Neo. Still, don't adopt a persona you can't live with. If blood makes you sick, don't become an ER doctor. Find a role that "rhymes with who you are on your best day". Don't use authenticity as an excuse to be a jerk.
  • On "give people what they want": careful, some people want heroin. Leadership means giving people what they'll come to realize they wanted all along. "Henry Ford did not make faster buggies."

⚖️ The 3 Ways to Make Change Happen

"You haven't made an impact until you've changed someone." Marketing is about making a change happen, even just moving a customer from store A to store B. "If you're not making a change happen, why are you bothering me?"

1. Create (and relieve) tension

  • When a hot new actor appears, every casting director faces tension. When a performer comes to town: "Should I get a ticket? Will it sell out?" No tension means no gap, so no one goes. A cardiologist saying "quit smoking or you'll probably die" creates useful, generous tension.
  • An amplifier maker (transcribed as "Deck Wear", a couple thousand dollars per unit) got a great review. The owner chose not to scale, so there's a 2-year waiting list on a shared Google Sheet where you can see your place. The waitlist became its own tension loop.
  • "The freelancer who says 'what do you need' is basically looking for a job without a boss." Saying "this is what I do and all I do" brings fewer yeses, but those people want in. It only works if you make something great first. You can't make cheap pizza and claim you only have 10 slices.
  • Be specific: "I only do weddings in these three zip codes. I'm the best, I charge the most, and I sign my work." No one waits in line for a supermarket that's like all the others.
  • The HVAC story: Godin's 100+ year-old house lost its heating. Of four contractors from the Yellow Pages, the first took off his shoes ("always") and handed Godin a clipboard with the names and numbers of 40 neighbors who'd agreed to be references. He was hired on the spot. Godin didn't even call the other three.
  • From the book's steps: (1) invent a thing worth making, with a story worth telling and a contribution worth talking about. (2) Design it so a few people will particularly benefit from it and care about it. It's almost never "spectacular". A juggler doesn't need 10 balls, because people hire you for how you make them feel. Penn & Teller and David Blaine aren't the best magicians, but there's only one of each.

2. Establish cultural norms and affiliation: "people like us do things like this"

  • Almost nobody goes first. You heard about the iPhone or this podcast from someone else. Walking around talking into earbuds would have looked insane 30 years ago and is normal now.
  • You don't wear a clown suit to a funeral. The "wedding industrial complex" has endless rules. People who'd never eat bad salmon with loud music want exactly that at their wedding because everyone else does.
  • Norms now change fast: "OK boomer" was high status, but saying it a year and a half too late is low status. Even accounting has insider rules.
  • Solar panels: the best predictor that a house has solar panels is whether the neighbors do. It has nothing to do with climate or geography.

3. See status roles and shift them

  • Status "has nothing to do with money, nothing to do with luxury". Examples: a Brooklyn restaurant owner greeting you by name in front of your brother. The crossing guard waving back. Guests at the Park Slope Food Co-op wearing a bright orange badge. Danny Meyer's system that flags regulars. His regulars revolted when he tried to drop tipping, because tipping gives them status.
  • Dermatologists wear stethoscopes as a badge. Someone "wins" every handshake. Bosses call meetings to take attendance. Varsity teams and play tryouts exist for status. Someone cutting you off on the exit ramp feels like losing the oasis. "We didn't invent the status game. Darwin did."
  • A great third-grade teacher gives status to the kid who never gets it: "Oh Billy, yeah, that's right." Praise from AI means less because it doesn't match the story we've had for years: your parents approve of you.
"What humans want is status, affiliation, and the freedom from fear. Those three things are the hole we're trying to fill for them." Elsewhere he adds convenience as a fourth thing marketers sell.

🛻 Case Study: Why the Cybertruck Failed

  • Model S worked because it "broke" the LA cardiologist's Mercedes. It was safer, faster and just as luxurious, so it bought status and affiliation, and became the best-selling luxury car in California within a couple of years.
  • The Ford F-150 is America's best-selling vehicle, "more than 100% of Ford's profits" per Godin. Pickup buyers want to fit in: sensible, useful, "I'm a working person".
  • The Cybertruck says "look at me". It's poorly designed, costs twice what it should and is ugly. Godin calls it "the worst car ever made" and a bigger money-loser than the Edsel. So the early adopters it needed never came and it never went mainstream.
  • He thinks a $45,000, nearly invisible, highly practical electric pickup would have sold millions. The fix: spend three days at a Ford dealership listening to couples. No one says "I want a purple one."
  • Lesson: marketing problems often come from a story that doesn't match the customer's own story about themselves.

🎯 Smallest Viable Audience vs. Chasing Virality

  • Being vague protects you from rejection, like saying you'd go to the prom with any of 100 people. Being specific puts you on the hook. Make work for one group and tell critics "it wasn't for you". A bass-fishing podcast can ignore people who want something else.
  • The Dip (96 pages, about quitting): people said it was too short and that nobody writes about quitting. "It's not for you." Never read your one-star reviews or YouTube comments. No author ever got better from them.
  • "Be a meaningful specific, not a wandering generality" (Zig Ziglar). Examples: a downtown NYC sit-down pizza place that serves only pizza and olives. Apollonia Poilâne's Paris bakery won't sell a baguette, even to tourists. Patagonia tells shoppers who can't afford it to "call Columbia". How often do you tell people "please go down the street"?
  • Calum's story: he took his brothers to Apollo Bagels, which has a small menu with nothing they wanted, so they left. That's healthy self-selection. His first clothing brand never launched because he kept trying to please everyone.
  • Vanity metrics: "Online friends are not your friends. Online followers are not your followers." The metrics were invented "to get you to work for free for Mark". Chasing them is why everything looks the same.
  • 1,000 true fans (Kevin Kelly): 1,000 people paying $100 each means you never need to look for customers again. Most people struggling don't have 3. Start by getting 10 and see if they tell others. From their earlier talk: needing 40 million views to make $200 is a tough spot.
  • Escaping the virality loop: name the game as rigged, and admit it's about "winning high school". "Real winning is being of service, making a promise to someone, keeping it." A doctor does great with 300 regular patients, while a dermatologist who needs 10M YouTube views is always hustling. Aim for people who would miss you if you were gone.

📣 Engineering Word of Mouth and Showing Up for Years

  • "You don't tell their friends, they tell their friends." Purple Cow was self-published (5,000 copies) and reached 1 million. Readers didn't spread it because they liked Seth. They spread it because their job would go better if their boss or coworkers read it. Spreading it served them.
  • Patagonia began selling small metal pegs to climbers, a smallest viable audience of about 10,000, and is now a billion-dollar company. The logo on the fleece (like TOMS shoes) lets wearers signal who they are.
  • Carmine's (Broadway & 90th, NYC) built word of mouth in on purpose: (1) reservations only for parties of 6 or more, (2) huge amounts of garlic, so you have to explain the smell at work the next day, (3) portions twice the usual size. That built a multi-million-dollar chain.
  • Steps 4 and 5: spread the word, then the often-overlooked one: "show up regularly, consistently, and generously for years and years". AI makes this more important. Unless you sell frontier models, you don't need every release. "The news changes every day, but the newspaper doesn't." Look for subscribers, and find products for your people instead of people for your products.

✅ Final Advice for Builders Who Are Stuck

  • Looking for "the one thing to do this weekend" keeps you stuck. "You're stuck because you're choosing to be stuck". Maybe it's baggage, too many goals, too big an ask for the trust you have, too much or too little hurry, or a problem that isn't real.
  • Go solve a problem for one person. Get paid for it ("generous doesn't mean free"). Become someone who solves problems, then repeat. You don't need your name on it at first. Don't quit your day job.
  • He admits the world isn't fair and that he "won the birthday lottery". Luck plays a big part. The question is still where you'll show up to make a difference for someone.

Key takeaways

  • Learn AI tools so AI works for you, then spend real time with customers to understand their fears and status needs.
  • Pick a specific, small audience. Make something great for them, turn away everyone else, and build in a reason to talk about you.
  • Ignore vanity metrics. Count true fans, keep your promises consistently, and show up for years.
