🔬 Part 2 — A Scalable RL Recipe for High Reliability
⚙️ Why Standard RL Doesn't Transfer Directly to Robotics

In language models, algorithms like PPO and GRPO scale to massive models and enable complex reasoning — but they need millions or tens of millions of attempts. That's fine when "attempting" just means running a forward pass in a GPU cluster.


The robot math problem: If you translated this naively — even just 1 million attempts at a 1-minute task — you'd need 700 robot-days of continuous operation. That's 2 years of a single robot doing nothing else. Not viable.

So Physical Intelligence redesigned RL for robotics with two key efficiency improvements:

🛠️ Efficiency Fix 1 — Human Interventions to Eliminate Dead-Ends

Standard RL wastes enormous time on dead-end trajectories — paths that can't possibly lead to success. In a data center this is just wasted compute. On a real robot, it's wasted hours and hardware wear.


The fix: A human monitors the robot and intervenes the moment it goes wrong — either showing it how to recover, or terminating the episode early and restarting.


Example — cardboard box stacking: The robot accidentally grabs two boxes flush together. If left alone, it would spend minutes trying to fold two boxes simultaneously — useless training data. Instead, a human teleoperates in, separates the boxes, and either lets the robot try to continue autonomously from the corrected state, or intervenes again if needed.


This means every second of robot time generates useful training data instead of noise.

🧠 Efficiency Fix 2 — A General-Purpose Value Function

In standard PPO/GRPO, for every single prompt the algorithm rolls out 10–50 attempts to estimate: what's a good response vs. a bad response? That cost multiplier explodes in robotics.


The fix: Instead of estimating value per-task, train a single general-purpose value function on large amounts of diverse robot video. This value function learns:

  • When a shirt is being unfolded while the task is to fold it → bad, negative progress
  • When a fold is proceeding correctly → good, positive progress
  • When retrieving an item from a fridge is going well → good (completely different task, same value function)

This "time-to-success" predictor amortizes the cost of value estimation across all tasks simultaneously, dramatically reducing the number of attempts needed to learn from experience.

🔄 The Full RL Loop
  1. Pre-train a foundation model on diverse robot manipulation data
  2. Collect autonomous experience — robot attempts the task, human intervenes at dead-ends
  3. Train the general-purpose value function on the collected experience
  4. Use the value function to update the policy — upweight good trajectories, downweight bad
  5. Repeat — each iteration produces a more reliable model

🧩 Part 3 — Memory: The Missing Ingredient for Long-Horizon Tasks
💾 Why Most Robot Models Have No Memory

Almost all state-of-the-art foundation models for robotics operate as pure reactive policies — they take the current camera frames as input and output the next action. No memory of what happened before.


For short repetitive tasks, this is fine. But for any multi-step sequential task — "make a full meal", "assemble a product from parts", "clean up a kitchen" — the robot needs to track what steps it has completed. Without memory, it can't.


Why is memory technically hard? A naive approach: feed in the last 10 seconds of video as context. At 50Hz control frequency × 4 cameras × 256 tokens/image = half a million tokens per forward pass. Even subsampling to 1fps = 10,000 tokens for just 10 seconds. This is prohibitively expensive at real-time inference speeds.


Physical Intelligence's solution: They developed a multi-timescale memory architecture — storing compressed representations at multiple temporal resolutions, not raw frames. Chelsea couldn't share full technical details in the talk, but the system enables long-horizon multi-step task execution that was previously impossible.
🚀 Part 4 — PIO7: A Single Model That Does Everything
🏗️ The Two Goals: Out-of-the-Box + Compositional Generalization

PIO7 is Physical Intelligence's latest foundation model. Two design goals:

  1. Out-of-the-box performance — the model should work on new tasks immediately without fine-tuning, the way GPT works on new questions without retraining
  2. Compositional generalization — the model should combine skills in novel ways it was never explicitly trained on, the way DALL-E can generate "a photo of an astronaut riding a horse" despite never seeing that combination

Training approach: PIO7 is trained on a single unified model controlling robots jointly predicting:

  • Target joint positions (angles of each robot joint)
  • Target end-effector position in 3D space
  • Future "imagination" frames — what should the scene look like after the next action? (sub-goal image conditioning)

Plus, the model is prompted with metadata about data quality — letting it extract useful signal even from lower-quality demonstrations.

✅ Goal 1: Out-of-the-Box Performance Matches Specialists

The key question: Can a single general model match a model that was specifically fine-tuned with RL for a specific task?


Result: Yes. PIO7 out-of-the-box matches or outperforms the fine-tuned PIO6 specialists — both RL-post-trained and SFT specialists — on every task measured, including espresso making, box building, and laundry folding. The analogy Chelsea draws: going from BERT (powerful but needs fine-tuning for every task) to GPT (works broadly out of the box).

Demonstrated in a single model: folding a collared shirt, inserting and drilling a screw into a robot arm, replacing a trash bag in a trash can — all diverse, precise, dexterous tasks.

Test 1 — Rare appliance: Air fryer. The training dataset contained essentially no air fryer data (after analysis, 3 accidental episodes found, expected to be negligible). Test 2 — New robot platform: BARM industrial robot. The team had folding data collected on their standard bimanual robot. They wanted to know: can PIO7 fold clothes on a completely different, much larger industrial robot — with different joint configurations, different link lengths, different kinematics — with zero folding data collected on that platform?

It took a few attempts (expected — literally its first time), but it got there. The team had never seen this kind of cross-embodiment generalization demonstrated before.

Quantitatively: as models scaled toward PIO7, performance on folding tasks on the new robot platform approached human teleop performance despite zero robot-specific training data for that task on that platform.

🔍 Ablation: What Actually Drives Generalization?

Chelsea presented ablation experiments to identify what made PIO7 generalize:

  • Diverse data is critical: Removing the most diverse subset of training data caused performance on held-out tasks to drop dramatically. Removing a random 20% of less-diverse data barely changed performance. Lesson: breadth of experience matters more than volume.
  • Metadata prompting unlocks low-quality data: Without metadata prompting about data quality, adding more low-quality data to the training mix decreased performance. With metadata prompting, adding that same low-quality data increased performance. The model learned to discount bad demos and learn from good ones. This is a significant practical result — it means you don't have to be as selective about what data you collect.
  • Imagination (sub-goal images) helps, but less than expected: PIO7's ability to generate and condition on imagined future frames (what should the scene look like after the next action?) improves performance measurably — but the base model without imagination was already so strong that they felt compelled to highlight the overall model rather than that specific capability.

💬 Q&A Highlights
How far are we from a "ChatGPT moment" for robotics?
Chelsea thinks the distribution moment won't look the same — you need physical hardware to deploy, which slows rollout (like Waymo's impressive but gradual expansion). But capability-wise, "we are really starting to get to the point where these models are actually useful in the real world" — a ChatGPT-level capability moment for robotics is "very much on the horizon in the next few years."
When should a small team switch from task-specific models to a generalist policy?
Right away, even from day one. Just start with a generalist policy and fine-tune it. PIO0 and PIO5 are open source and already powerful. The only exception: extremely constrained environments (e.g., surgical robots in basement operating rooms with no internet and a bad GPU — though even then, local inference on a workstation is feasible).
Is there an internet-scale dataset equivalent for robotics?
The robotics equivalent of internet text is robot experience data — and unlike text, you can't just scrape it. Short term: teleoperated demonstrations. Long term: autonomous robot experience generated by deployed robots attempting tasks in the real world (synthetic data loops, like how LLMs now generate their own training data). Human video (YouTube, etc.) is also useful context — but robots need their own embodied experience. "Watching Roger Federer play tennis doesn't mean I can play like him."
Will general-purpose robotics models be democratized via open source?
Harder than LLMs because data collection requires physical hardware. But Chelsea is optimistic about a strong open-source community — π already open-sourced PIO0 and PIO5, and even closed-source companies tend to support open ecosystems (e.g., Gemma from Google, GPT open-weights). "I don't know if it will play out exactly the way language models did, but I'm optimistic."
Raw motor commands, joint positions, or end-effector targets — what does the model output?
All demonstrated models output target joint positions (angles) and target 3D gripper positions, then a PD controller closes the loop to actually hit those targets. Not raw voltages or torques, though there are pros and cons. Raw voltages would let the model control joint stiffness, which could matter in some tasks. Chelsea says joint-angle control doesn't appear to be a bottleneck currently, so she focuses on what is.
Do robots need "imagination" — picturing what should happen next — to be truly useful?
PIO7 has this: it predicts sub-goal images (what the scene should look like after the next action) and conditions on them. It helps measurably. But the base model without imagination was already so strong that Chelsea isn't certain imagination is a critical ingredient vs. a useful one. "Hard to say if it'll necessarily be a critical component. Empirically so far it seems to help — although perhaps not as much as you might expect."
Robots are impressive but slow — how do you improve speed?
RL training already produced 2× throughput improvement. Another π release ("RL token") showed speeds faster than human teleoperation. The bottleneck: human teleoperators are slow, so the training data is slow. Two paths forward: make the data-collection faster, or find ways to generalize beyond the speed of training data. "We've seen evidence of being able to be faster than the data. More to come."
Most surprising robot behavior you've seen recently?
During PIO7 training on a pinwheel assembly task, all training data showed the robot picking the pin with its right hand and the paper with its left. The robot made a mistake — paper ended up on the right, pin on the left. The robot spontaneously switched hands and completed the task correctly — inserting the pin with its left gripper, which appeared in zero training data. It had learned emergent left-right equivariance — transferring a skill from one hand to the other without ever being shown it. "I don't know if other people were as excited about it as I was… but it shows emergent capability I hadn't seen before."
Is a PhD worth it for someone going into robotics/industry?
Chelsea wasn't planning to do one — her engineer father even said he wouldn't hire PhDs (different field, civil engineering). But she now values it deeply: a PhD teaches you how to handle uncertainty — how to pick good problems, how to navigate not knowing if your research direction will pan out in 6 months or 10 years. That skill is invaluable at AI frontiers where nobody knows the right path. That said, there are also enormous opportunities in industry-side engineering (robot software stacks, data infrastructure, ML systems) where a PhD isn't needed. Very personal decision.
