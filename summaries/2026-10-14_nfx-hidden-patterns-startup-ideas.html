🧭 The Idea Landscape

  • Fertile soil (right): a 2–3 person team that goes hard for two years ends up selling for $10M+.
  • "I'm not sure" (middle): e.g. Airbnb — he didn't think people would sleep on an air bed in a stranger's room.
  • Mediocre (left): about 65% of the time he can tell an idea will waste your life's energy.
  • His job is to move founders from left to right; theirs is to execute. The talk is about big, world-changing companies, not bakeries.

📐 Patterns in the Idea Itself

1. Network effects

  • NFX's study: about 70–72% of the value created in tech over the last 30 years came from companies with network effects at their core.
  • An enterprise SaaS company can sell for $1B, but it's very hard to reach $10B without network effects. NFX has mapped 16 types (see nfx.com). Design them in before you start.

2. The three S's: simple, scalable, similar

  • Simple: "The A students work for the B students and the C students make all the money" — because they simplify. Smart people over-complicate; startups often only make the right call when they're down to their last ~$400K or 60 days of cash, and he sometimes lets portfolio companies get there on purpose.
  • Scalable: especially on the distribution side — software, biology, technology.
  • Similar: the biggest companies were very similar to something that had just started working a few years before.

3. "Copy a lot, but not all" — the new-energy zone

  • Totally new ideas get praise but no buyers. Pure clones get sued and lose community support (cloning in another geography is fine).
  • The sweet spot: something that already works plus 2–3 obvious changes.
  • Lending Club vs. Prosper: Lending Club changed two things — bundled the loans and talked to the FTC before launching — reached about a $7B valuation, while Prosper was temporarily shut down. Same pattern: Google vs. Infoseek, Airbnb vs. VRBO, Facebook vs. Friendster (one of ~100 social networks at the time).

4. Market risk vs. execution risk

  • First-time founders often take both risks — and are bad at execution because they've never done it. Second-timers take only execution risk.
  • Elon Musk after X.com/PayPal: no more market risk — people already drive cars, launch payloads and buy solar panels.
  • List every risk (regulation, city-by-city rollout, international) and compare ideas. A Battery Ventures lesson: international deals add a whole extra set of risks — he lost 100% on two such investments. Medical software has a long record of losing money.

5. Red ocean over blue ocean

  • Blue oceans carry more market risk. Founders in red oceans usually do better: you know someone buys it, how to price it and how to sell it. "Get some blood into the system" — revenue and customers first, then learn.
  • Proof point: of 75 accelerator companies using this method, 8 are already unicorns.

6. Timing: rapid platform shifts

  • Look for rapid technology platform shifts where something is suddenly possible — e.g. generative tech, whose price dropped ~100x in about eight months (as stated in the talk). Earlier examples: mobile, blockchain.
  • Cultural shifts ("Gen Z buys differently") take a decade, so everyone sees them — rarely an opening.
  • Regulatory shifts: act after the change, not before (home dialysis: the opening lasted ~4 years after the law changed). Don't bet on pending legislation — it slips.
  • "Technology in search of a market" rarely works unless the tech is itself a platform with multiple shots on goal (how NFX invests in biotech).
  • Prefer a fast-growing market, even if small today. Don't just digitize old B2B transactions; create new transactions in a sub-segment.

7. Users × price per user (the "boundary layer")

  • From his games company: FarmVille made ~$0.04/day from ~100M people; his own attempt reached ~30M users — not enough. Dragons of Atlantis had ~50K users at almost $4/day — a real business. Mafia Wars: ~80M users at ~$2.70/day drove Zynga.
  • The area under the curve must be big. A tool for ~10–50K game developers tends to become a $50–100M acquisition — fine, but not a giant company.
  • Games tip: themes that work are the Lego aisle — pirates, mafia, Atlantis, city builders.

🌎 Ecosystems and Where Ideas Come From

  • Centers of excellence and company mafias produce founders: Facebook, PayPal, Stripe, Square, Salesforce, PeopleSoft, Unit 8200.
  • The Bay Area is at least ~7x better than any other ecosystem: you can hire a great employee #68, get advice from people with scars, and pick up shared KPIs (e.g. an email tweak lifting click-through from 2% to 7%). Remote doesn't replace this — he expects the back-to-office trend to continue in competitive, creative fields.
  • Customer-discovery frameworks (the kind consultants sell to governments and corporations) rarely produce great companies: customers can't see the future. Dock workers would ask for better shoes, not a crane. He doesn't advocate full Steve Jobs mode, but sits closer to it than "ask customers what they want".
  • Breakthroughs often come from non-users: start with a new transaction for a specific person.
  • Example of for-profit impact: entrepreneurs who moved used European GSM mobile networks to Africa and transformed economies. His closing point: be a speedy, competitive founder first; the mission comes once the business works.

💡 Conclusions

  • Most idea failures are predictable from patterns; pick the idea carefully before optimizing for speed.
  • The winning recipe is usually a proven model + 2–3 meaningful changes + network effects + a better team, timed to a platform shift.
  • Conventional advice (go to blue oceans, ask customers what they want, chase huge current TAMs) is often wrong for big outcomes.

🎯 Recommendations

  • Before building, check the idea against: network effects, the three S's, the risk list, ocean color, timing and users × price.
  • Copy something that works and change 2–3 things; consider cloning a proven model in another geography.
  • Start small (the skateboard), keep beliefs loosely held, and pivot on data.
  • Recruit commandos and people better than you; be where the ecosystem is strongest.
