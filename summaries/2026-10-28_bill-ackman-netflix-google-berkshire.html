🤖 AI: Bubble, Existential Risk, Capex

Is it a bubble?

  • No bubble in demand for compute. We're "asymptotically approaching superintelligence", and demand for it will be "something approaching infinity". Being long compute is a good trade.
  • He's more cautious about neoclouds and new financing structures with "a fair degree of leverage". The big players (Microsoft is AAA-rated) will be sophisticated with their credit.

A threat to humanity?

  • "There's real risk." Models will become more brain-like: efficient, activating only part of the network, learning recursively. Silicon instead of carbon. Model consciousness is "possible" (citing an NYT piece on Anthropic).
  • Most frightening: people at the labs don't fully understand how the models learn, and models "have this habit of not staying within the sandbox". The Terminator scenario is "possible", especially alongside fast robotics progress (a robotic finger with growing human skin).
  • Regulation: don't slow down law-abiding companies while rule-breakers race ahead. "The way we'll save ourselves from AI is probably a better AI, and hopefully we have it as opposed to one of our enemies."
  • US vs. China: "definitely an arms race." The next war will be decided by swarms of autonomous drones (air, sea, underground), not aircraft carriers.
  • On Anthropic's S-1, he had tweeted that it would need a risk factor saying "we may destroy all of humanity".

Hyperscaler capex

  • Amazon and Microsoft cloud were never capital-light. What changed is a prolonged shift in the demand curve, not a spike. When demand is huge, sustainable and hard to build for, pricing holds, so heavy spending is prudent.
  • They aren't pure-play hyperscalers; their core businesses use compute and benefit from being at the frontier.
  • The capex-driven drops in Meta, Microsoft and Amazon were buying opportunities. "I think those stocks are still cheap."

🏦 Markets, Rates, Inflation & Policy

  • Waiting for a crash: the opportunity cost of waiting makes it "a very difficult way to make money". Find enduring high-quality compounders instead of predicting short-term moves. Not as fun as get-rich-quick, but a better long-term outcome.

🧭 Investment Philosophy

"What we try to do is buy the best… super durable growth companies in the world — businesses that have very wide moats."
  • The key question: what's the risk this business is disrupted by "a 19-year-old who left Stanford"? Even Buffett praised newspapers for years and couldn't foresee the internet. AI makes disruption much harder to predict.
  • Recipe: very high-certainty businesses, low disruption risk, fast growth, bought at a price that gives a high return. You rarely get all of that without an event. In the past that meant management mistakes plus a disappointed seller. Now it's usually analysts disliking a capex budget, or a narrative you believe the market has wrong.
  • Black-swan hedging: occasional asymmetric hedges (CDS, options) against market-crash events. There have been three in 22 years, each hedged differently.
  • No longer an activist (no activist investment in ~a decade). Early on, nobody knew him and managements pushed back, so proxy fights, letters and presentations were needed, and most worked. Boards now take activists seriously, and after 23 years Pershing is a known quantity. For 4 of 6 recent new positions, the CEO wrote a thank-you note. Ideas now get discussed in the boardroom, not on CNBC, and the quality of businesses he can own has improved.

What makes a business almost impossible to disrupt

  • Microsoft: ~half a billion people working in M365 at ~$25/seat, with trust built on security. It's a pipeline for selling ever-better Copilot AI.
  • Being an essential part of everyone's day, like the Bloomberg terminal in finance.
  • Uber: the bear case is that autonomous vehicles (Elon's Cybercab) kill it. Ackman thinks there will be many AV companies, cheaper rides and more demand, aggregated by a platform. Not every AV maker will have its own app.
  • Restaurant Brands (QSR): brand and market position. Origin: Pershing took Burger King public via its own SPAC (2012) and pitched the Tim Hortons acquisition. It knew Tims from its earlier Wendy's activism, where it took a ~10% stake and pushed the Tim Hortons spin-off. Brand test: "if it's managed poorly but still exists". Burger King survived decades of mismanagement. Franchising is a capital-light, inflation-protected royalty, and its price point holds up in tough economies.

Why concentration

  • There aren't many great businesses at sensible prices at any moment. A 100-stock portfolio would have "85–90 that were not as good as the top 10". Adding a name to a 12-stock portfolio barely improves diversification, and "you're forced to own your 12th best idea as opposed to more of your best idea."

📺 Netflix: Sold, Then Bought Back · 🔍 Alphabet: Sold on Price

Netflix (2022 exit)

  • He bought after a subscriber miss crushed the stock, believing Netflix would be the long-term streaming winner with great management.
  • He met management soon after, which "probably was a mistake in retrospect". They agreed with everything, and when he suggested advertising they said "we will never do this". Three weeks later they missed badly, looked "shell shocked", and said they were considering ads.
  • He lost confidence in the predictability of the outcome and the spread of outcomes widened, so as a "very high certainty investor" he sold (around $275). The capital went into Google when ChatGPT launched and it looked behind.
  • Netflix then fell to ~$175 ("probably should have bought it back then") and later ran past $1,000.

Netflix (2026 re-entry)

  • Worries about US viewing time cut the stock in half. Now Netflix has a successful ad model, a clear #1 position and much stronger free cash flow, making it a lower-risk business at an attractive entry price (~50% off levels of a few months earlier).

Alphabet vs. Microsoft/Amazon/Meta

  • "Principally price." Alphabet rose to where the forward return fell below Pershing's long-term target. Meta was bought at a "very attractive entry price".
  • They discussed Meta's Muse: Meta jumped ~20% the week after launch while travel and e-commerce stocks fell, as the market priced in Muse finding users better deals.
  • "The library": a watchlist of a few hundred admired companies, updated when a $200–250B company drops 30–40% on a guidance miss. No Australian stocks; he's North America-centric ("Australia is just very far away"), though he wants to visit the Australian Open.

Berkshire's secret & today's Berkshire

  • Berkshire wasn't a pure insurer but a diversified holding company. That made it easy to stop writing insurance when pricing was bad (the capital had other uses) and made it more creditworthy, since many businesses weren't correlated with insurance.
  • On today's Berkshire: to move the needle it should do deals needing ~$100B of capital, not $1–5B. It sits on hundreds of billions in cash, so it's "well positioned if the world blows up". The insurance business remains excellent.
  • On Greg Abel: a very high-quality person and a good choice, known more as an operator than an investor. At a moment when writing $100B checks is the differentiator, buying securities is less of an edge for Berkshire.

Howard Hughes → "modern-day Berkshire Hathaway"

  • The story starts with General Growth: a Class A mall owner that over-used CMBS leverage before the financial crisis. It couldn't refinance and the stock fell 99%, though its malls were ~95% occupied. Pershing bought ~25% and Ackman joined the board, arguing assets exceeded liabilities so shareholders could keep their equity through Chapter 11.
  • Howard Hughes today owns "small cities": essentially all the undeveloped commercial and residential land in master-planned communities. It builds infrastructure and sells lots to homebuilders, and the new residents drive demand for its shopping centers, apartments and offices. Communities: The Woodlands (~60 years), Summerlin (Las Vegas, early '90s), Bridgeland (Houston), plus a ~60-acre beachfront parcel in Hawaii for condos.
  • It doesn't fit the REIT mold (no dividends, lots of development and land), so it has been an "orphaned, very cheap stock".
  • A Forbes cover a decade ago said Bill would make HHH the next Berkshire. The stock hit ~$160, nothing happened, and it fell back. ("Never be on the cover of Forbes.")
  • The plan, launched ~1.5 years ago: Pershing bought an additional stake with ~$900M of capital. HHH will monetize real-estate assets (a process being accelerated) and redeploy the proceeds Berkshire-style.

Why insurance is the key

  • The insurance platform is being built while avoiding pre-2020 liabilities ("some scary stuff" in that era).
  • Traditional insurers hold ~90–100% fixed income. Equity capital charges are higher, and great stock pickers go to hedge funds, Fidelity or Citadel because insurers can't match their pay. So insurers ignore the asset side.
  • Doing both (a high return on assets plus low-cost float from good underwriting) "is basically what Buffett did". Buffett's National Indemnity was an ~$18M purchase. HHH's Vantage deal was ~$2B: a relatively small start.

🏠 High-Conviction Bet: Fannie Mae & Freddie Mac

  • "A royalty on the US mortgage market." Seized by the government in the financial crisis, they have since rebuilt capital and "should be released from conservatorship". They still trade on the OTC pink sheets.

✅ Advice for Everyday Investors

  • Do your homework and build a list of businesses you want to own. Eventually, through a war, a macro event or a bad quarter, one becomes available at a really attractive price.
  • Buy it and never sell: let it compound tax-free. "That to me is the best way to make money."
  • Individuals have permanent capital. Money you won't need for 5+ years is a real edge over "a guy who works for Citadel [who's] got to make money next month, otherwise he's toast."
  • Don't try to time the market; the opportunity cost of waiting is high.
  • Ask of every holding: what's the risk a Stanford dropout disrupts it? Prefer capital-light, royalty-like businesses with brands that survive bad management.
